# Scope research: what to make in the Roblox-like Shorts space (2026-11-02)

The goal of this round was to work out what kinds of Roblox-style Shorts are worth making, which parts of the space are crowded, and which might be open. It covers four things:

- **A.** A map of the main Roblox-style Short formats on YouTube.
- **B.** Hit rates for 16 channels over the last three months, including channels that started recently.
- **C.** A check of the "open ground" ideas from earlier notes (transport and night places).
- **D.** Notes on Roblox branding and YouTube "made for kids" rules.

Everything here was read only. Nothing was liked, followed, subscribed to or commented on.

## Method and caveats (read these first)

- **Data source.** YouTube search with the Shorts filter. For each query I took the top results by views and pulled each Short's publish date, channel and length from its page.
- **Time window.** "In window" means published from 2026-08-02 to 2026-10-30. Shorts posted on Oct 31 or later were left out because they hadn't had time to collect views.
- **Channel stats** come from each channel's Shorts tab, using every Short in the window: median views, the share with 1M+ views, and the share under 100K.
- **Search shows winners.** A query with "0 at 1M+" means none showed up in the top results. It does **not** prove there are no hits. A query with many hits mostly tells you that a few big channels have it covered.
- **This is a snapshot.** View counts change daily, and October numbers are still growing.
- **Some searches were flaky.** A few queries ("roblox facts / what happens if", "in this world animation (non-roblox)") returned 0 cards, probably a YouTube glitch. Treat those as untested, not as empty.
- **Survivorship bias.** I could look up channels I found through hits, but the many small channels that never break out barely show up in search. The three small channels in section B are the only check against this.

**What this says**

- **Established channels hit almost every time.** Gneef, Noobified, PurpleHat and ItsGuestCap get 1M+ on about 90% of uploads. The audience is still large and is not shrinking: Gneef's October was its best month.
- **New channels are still breaking out**, so the market isn't locked:
  - PurpleHat joined in June 2026 and is at 583K subs.
  - Baseplate joined in February 2026 and is at 675K subs.
  - Gelo posted about 100 Shorts in October alone and got 46% to 1M+.
  - BloxyRiddles and Revan both jumped about 10× from September to October.
- **Breakouts are sudden.** BloxyRiddles had a September median of 209K and an October median of 2.0M. Revan went from 309K to 4.9M. This looks like the algorithm "finding" a channel after a run of uploads, not slow growth.
- **The other side of that:** Blox Park, Rilo and Spok are at 23–45K medians after 4–12 uploads. That is roughly where we are. Each of these channels has posted fewer than a dozen Shorts. Volume looks like a real factor: Gelo posted about 100 and Gneef about 110 in October.
- **Cuby Bro is the best sign for us.** It is a small channel (67K subs) with only 11 Shorts, and half of them reached 1M+. Its top Shorts were "The Road Gets Longer" (4.2M) and "In This Town The Bus NEVER Stops" (1.3M). These are travel/place premises close to our creepy-lane ideas.

## The redistributor finding

Many of the round-1 Instagram "hits" are re-uploads of Gneef Shorts (titles and premises match):

- Steps (33M)
- String (30M)
- One Tower (26M)
- Teleport (20M)
- Never Turn Around (10M)
- Magic Line (10M)
- Shrink For Lying (4.1M)
- Balloon (1.8M)

**noobifieed** on Instagram is very likely re-posting Noobified.

So the Instagram accounts we studied are mostly redistributors, not originators. The originals are a small number of YouTube channels. Two consequences:

- When we model "what works," we should look at Gneef, Noobified and PurpleHat directly, not at the IG copies.
- Instagram view counts on these accounts partly reflect premises that were already proven on YouTube. That may inflate how well any given premise looks on IG.

**Reading this carefully**

- **Bus, train and elevator aren't open.** Big channels have done them in the last three months, with 2–8M results.
- **Gas station, subway and counting horror show no hits.** That could mean open ground, or it could mean there's little demand. Search can't tell these apart. The fact that many small channels tried them and none broke 0.1M is a mild warning sign, not a green light.
- **What actually made the hits:** the hits in these settings came from channels with a strong premise engine (Noobified, PurpleHat). The setting wasn't what made them work. So a setting like "gas station" probably isn't an angle on its own; it needs a premise ("In this town the gas station never…").

## D. Roblox-like vs. Roblox, branding and made-for-kids

I'm not a lawyer. These are things to check, not legal conclusions.

- **Roblox brand use.** Roblox's licensing program and brand guidelines limit using the Roblox name and logos in ways that could suggest an official connection or endorsement. Roblox's help article covers using Roblox content in videos, and creators do routinely make Roblox videos. Using "Roblox" in a brand or channel name is a separate, stricter question that people have asked about on the DevForum. In practice:
  - Our own blocky characters (not Roblox avatars or assets), no Roblox logo, and no "Roblox" in the channel name keeps us clear of the most obvious problems.
  - Almost every competitor uses #roblox and "roblox" in titles and tags. Whether we tag that way when our content isn't made in Roblox is a judgment call. It could get us in front of the right viewers, but it could also look misleading. **This is worth deciding on purpose.**
- **Look-alike vs. made in Roblox.** Everything in section B appears to be made in Roblox or styled exactly like it (R15-style avatars, Roblox UI cues). A "Roblox-like but ours" look (blocky, but our own proportions, faces and world) is untested against this audience. It's a bet on:
  - **Upside:** an owned style, brand safety, and room to grow beyond Roblox.
  - **Risk:** the recommendation system and viewers may group us with Roblox content less well.
- **Made for kids (COPPA).** YouTube requires every video to be marked for whether it's made for kids. The FTC's factors include the subject, animated characters, bright visuals and child actors. Content marked "made for kids" loses comments, personalized ads and some features, which usually means much lower earnings and less discovery through the normal Shorts feed. Our audience likely includes many children. The creepy lane, with mature-ish tension and teen/adult framing, is probably easier to argue as "general audience" than cute or silly content. Still, this needs a deliberate decision per video, not a default. The big Roblox story channels appear to be set as general audience (comments are on).

## Scope: what to make (hypotheses to test, not conclusions)

Given the overfitting concern, each of these is framed as a hypothesis with a cheap test.

1. **H1: The lane is still open to newcomers, but it rewards volume.**
   - Evidence: PurpleHat, Baseplate, Gelo, BloxyRiddles and Revan all broke out in 2026, while channels with fewer than 12 uploads sit at 20–45K.
   - Test: commit to a fixed batch (for example 20 Shorts in 4 weeks) before judging. Don't judge on 3–5 uploads.
2. **H2: The premise is the product, not the setting.**
   - Evidence: the winners use "In this town/world, [one rule]" engines. Bus and elevator worked for premise channels, while setting-only searches (gas station, subway) found nothing.
   - Test: write 10 one-line premises and score them blind before animating. Only produce the ones that pass a "would I tap this?" check by someone outside the project.
3. **H3: A creepy twist on a premise beats pure horror.**
   - Evidence: pure animated horror and night-bus searches peak under 1M. Gneef-style premises with an eerie edge reach 10–33M.
   - Test: alternate "eerie-premise" and "straight-horror" Shorts within the batch, and compare completion rate and views at day 7.
4. **H4: Our own look is a differentiator, not a handicap.** This is the riskiest and most important hypothesis.
   - Test: in the batch, make a few Shorts as close to the Roblox look as we're comfortable with and the rest in our own blocky style. Keep premises comparable. If our style consistently underperforms by a large margin, revisit.
5. **H5: A recurring format beats one-offs.**
   - Evidence: Noobified's Part 2s and Gelo's "In This Town…" series.
   - Test: give the best-performing premise from the batch a Part 2 within a week and check whether it beats the original's day-7 views.

**What to avoid, based on this data**

- Copying a specific Gneef premise. The audience has seen them, and Instagram has already recycled them.
- Pure jump-scare horror without a premise.
- Judging the channel on a handful of uploads.
