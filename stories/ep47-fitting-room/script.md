## The story

- **Premise:** In a town where every lie you tell pins a small white tag to your clothes, I've never had one. I'm back for a week to help Uncle Aurel shut the alterations shop where he taught me to cut. He has never told a lie in his life, but every time a customer asks after me, a tag pins onto him. By night the fitting-room curtain draws itself, my old bench gets chalked up for a suit, and a tape measure lies on the floor in the shape of a person where I used to stand.
- **Rule:** every lie you tell pins a white tag to your clothes. A tag only comes off when somebody says the truth out loud (shown in the montage: a man owns up in the cafe and his tag drops off like a leaf).
- **Twist:** a lie you believe never gets a tag. I never had one because for two years I'd told myself I was happy in the city, and believed it. Uncle knew all along. All his tags are the same one lie, "My nephew's happy in the city", told to every customer so I could stay away without guilt. "People believe everything I say" turns over too: they believe me because I believe myself.
- **Where the twist is planted:**

## World

An old covered shopping arcade in a small town: a long glass roof on curved iron ribs, sunlight in long bars across a cream-and-charcoal tiled floor, a row of small shopfronts in bottle-green painted wood with brass handles, a cafe with three little tables spilling out, a shoe shop, a hair salon. Every shop sign is a picture, never a word: a shoe, a cup, a comb, and for Uncle's shop a spool of thread with a needle through it.

Uncle's alterations shop is narrow and deep. The front: a wooden counter with a brass bell, a rail of finished clothes in brown paper covers, a big window onto the arcade. The back, by a second window onto a side passage: two heavy wooden cutting benches facing each other, each on four cast brass feet, each with its own green-shaded bench lamp. One is Uncle's, worn smooth. The second was mine, bare now. Along the back wall, two apron hooks (one empty), a fitting room with a heavy ochre curtain on brass rings, and a narrow stair up to the two rooms above where we sleep. Packing boxes stacked on the counter all week, more each day.

- **No text anywhere.** Signs are pictures. The tags carry only our hook glyph. The sign card on the door has a picture of a spool on one side and is plain on the other. The captions carry every word.
- **The marker: the tags.** Small rectangles of matte off-white card (`#efeae0`, never pure white), about the size of a matchbox, with one notched end and a tiny brass pin through the other. Each is printed with our **hook glyph** in black: one thick stroke that rises and curls over at the top into a coat hook, with a small open ring at its foot like a screw eye. It's not a letter or number in any alphabet we know. A tag pins on with a small soft pop of motion (a 0.3 s scale-up from nothing, no light change). It drops off by tipping and drifting down like a leaf.
- **What's true underneath (never said):** the shop was built for two: two benches, two hooks. When I left, the count went one short, and the shop's Keeper, the Empty, stands in my place: my bench, my measurements, my size of shoe. It doesn't want me or Uncle. It only fills the gap. The tags are only the town's rule and have nothing to do with it, so it carries none. When I step up to my bench to stay, the count is whole and the suit drops.
