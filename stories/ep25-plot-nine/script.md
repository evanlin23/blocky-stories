## The big idea

**The faceless thing climbing out of the allotments, one plot nearer each dawn, is Gran: Grandad is forgetting her a year at a time, and the hill gives each year back in the plot where they spent it.**

The rule ("whatever you forget comes back up") is the idea seen from inside. Every strange detail clicks at once in the reveal (L29 to L33): why it rises in plots one, four and seven (the three stakes Grandad says hello to every morning, their old plots), why it faces down the path toward plot nine (their plot now), why it has no face (the face is what goes first), why its roots are knotted under the chin (Gran's headscarf), why it rises higher each dawn (more of her forgotten), why Grandad isn't scared of it (some part of him knows her), and why the holes it leaves have no bottom (the years it came up from).

### Ten pitches, one kept
1. **Kept: the forgotten come back up, and the path is their life.** Above.
2. Every seed takes fifty years to come up, and what's rising in plot nine is what Grandad planted the year he met Gran. Warm, but no "oh no".
3. Rain falls up out of the ground, because there's an upside-down allotment under ours. A "wait, what" with no one to love.
4. The whole hill is the cupped hand of something huge, and the holes are where its fingers were. Big scale, no feeling.
5. The narrator is the old man with the failing memory, and the "newcomer" is family he doesn't know. Too close to the owner's coma example (the narrator's own condition explains the world), and easy to confuse.
6. Gardeners who stay too long take root and become the hedges. Too close to ep55's moss.
7. The hill is a terrarium on Grandad's windowsill, and the drought is him forgetting to water it. The snow-globe twist is worn out.
8. It only rains on plots whose holder somebody misses. Too close to ep38 and ep43's "who misses whom" rules.
9. Ten plots and always ten gardeners, and I'm the eleventh. A count story, near stand-in.
10. Things you plant come up as whatever you were thinking when you planted them, and all of Grandad's come up as me. Sweet, small.

## The story

- **Premise:** On an allotment hill where whatever you forget comes back up out of the ground, where you left it, I'm the one gardener nothing ever comes up for. Grandad has plot nine across the path from mine. This week his glasses and his cap come up in his beans. Then a head and shoulders of soil and roots with no face comes up in plot one by the gate, and every dawn it's in a plot further down the path, higher out of the ground, nearer to his.
- **The rule (line 1):** "On this hill, whatever you forget comes back up out of the ground." 13 words.
- **The marker by 2 s:** forgotten things sprouting up out of the beds on green stalks, big and lit, with a face in frame: a bunch of keys on a stalk in a bed of lettuce, the newcomer (plot six) looking down at it. Odd and funny first, the eerie turn comes at L13.
- **The montage (L01 to L05, about 10 s):** keys growing in the lettuce into a "key plant". Nobody owns a second umbrella, because the handles sprout by the gate. Plot two grows flasks on a bush. Go and find it, and it's yours again: the newcomer wheels off with her keys on the handlebar.
- **The narrator is the exception (L06, L07):** nothing ever comes up in my plot, plot ten. I don't forget things. This is what turns the climax: I remember Grandad's stories when he can't.
- **The loved one at stake (L08 to L12, picture by about 15 s):** Grandad, on his upturned bucket at plot nine, across the path from me. What he could lose: Gran. He talks to his beans like she's still there, and her green scarf still hangs in his shed.
- **The loophole (planted L05, turned L34):** what comes back up is yours again if you go and find it. You can't pick up a person, but you can walk someone back to every place their years came up. I walk him up the path, stop at every hole, and tell him what he told me there. At plot nine he says her name, and the soil settles back down. No new magic: the rule's own second half.
- **Twist and how the last 15 s turn the first line over:** the first time, "whatever you forget comes back up" is a funny world rule about keys and umbrellas. After L30 it means a person, a whole marriage coming up a year at a time. Then the sting turns it once more, onto me: the one who never forgets finds something in their own plot and can't remember who it is (L41 to L43). A second watch hears L01 as Grandad's story, and L07 ("I don't FORGET things") as the line the ending breaks.
- **The two plants a rewatch catches:**
  - Plant 1, the stakes (L09, about 14.5 s): every morning Grandad says hello to three old stakes, "ONE, FOUR, SEVEN." The thing rises in exactly those plots, in that order. Paid at L31 ("ONE, FOUR, SEVEN, NINE. Every plot they've HAD").

  - Plant 3, small: Grandad "just goes QUIET" (L16) instead of being scared.

## World

The same allotment hillside, at dawn, in a dry spring. A wooden five-bar gate at the top with its chain and padlock (seen, never mentioned). One dusty path runs straight downhill. Ten plots, odd numbers on the left and even on the right, each with low board edges and a plain wooden stake at the path corner. Every bed but plot nine is pale cracked clay (the look, not narrated). Plot nine near the bottom on the left is dark worked soil with two rows of bean wigwams and Grandad's creosote-brown shed at its path corner. Plot ten, across the path from it, is mine: neat, bare, raked. Beyond the bottom fence the hill drops into a misty valley.

- **No text anywhere.** Stakes are plain wood, no numbers. The captions carry the plot numbers.
- **What's true underneath (never said):** on this hill, whatever you forget comes back up where you left it. A forgotten thing comes up as itself. A forgotten person comes up as soil and roots with their shape and no face, rising further out of the ground the more of them is forgotten, and it comes up a year at a time in the place each year was spent. When it's remembered, it settles back down. It's simply how this hill is. It has nothing to do with the Keepers, and no Keeper is in this story.
