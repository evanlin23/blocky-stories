## The 2026-11-03 reset: shorter, faster, lighter (outranks the story and packaging rules below)
The owner, 2026-11-03: "you can do whatever you want, this is your channel to experiment with." Why: the uploads after back-row flopped. Today's trend scan has "sad / emotional" as the weakest format, falling 14% in a week, while rule premises and horror lead. A blocky channel 24 days old reached 178K subscribers with seven 50 to 57 s Shorts (`docs/research/2026-11-03-skylox-study.md`). Our stories ran 130 to 145 s, mostly sad family reveals.
- **Length is an open question, so test it.** The new channel studied runs 50 to 57 s, but the top 10% of the trend scan runs a median 140 s (deep scan, 2026-11-03), and back-row hit at 2:18. Each batch splits between 60 to 90 s and 120 to 150 s until our own numbers decide. Length never rescues a weak first second.
- **Premise:** a relatable want or fight (a parent, sibling, best friend or partner, a wish anyone has had), plus a creepy or fun rule on top. Fun rules count, like a power or a price that comes with the rule. **At most one story in three ends on a sad family reveal.** The feeling stays, as a sting in the last seconds, not the whole story. The big idea still has to make someone say "wait, what".
- **First second:** the rule spoken plainly, over a close-up of what it's about (the marker, the object, the face). No establishing wide first.
- **The rule meter:** the story's own number, timer, mark or glow stays visible the whole time, so the rule never leaves the screen.
- **Pace:** a cut or punch-in every 1 to 2 s, and no still picture over 2 s.
- **Look:** bright and saturated by default, on the closer-to-Roblox look for every story. Dark and fog are for one scene, not the whole Short. Use the `assets/` props to fill sets.
- **Ending:** the last line leads straight back into the first, so a loop plays as one sentence.
- **Comments:** the pin asks for one easy word tied to the story ("Comment BELL if yours would have rung this week."). The comment job posts it.
- **Titles:** 3 to 7 words: the rule or the hook, one word in caps, one emoji at the end. "In This Town" only rarely. Never a spoiler.
- **Series experiment:** a recurring "me" and family across several Shorts that can mention each other. Each one still stands alone (a third of the experiment slots until we know).
- **Never copy:** the game's name or currency, "subscribe to get" promises, or any channel's premise, characters or titles. Hard rule 5 still applies, so vary the skeleton story to story.
- **Only good videos go up; sunk cost never counts** (owner, 2026-11-03: "We shouldn't upload anything that isnt good. If we find out that x things do well, we should ensure that all of our stuff follows, not say hey we've already rendered/a lot of work. it will be more harmful if we uplaod soemthing that performs poorly right?"). A weak upload costs more than a skipped day: each one trains the feed on the channel and drags its averages down. So:
  - **2026-11-03: no story merges without a passed proof qa.** The owner, 2026-11-03: "why are stuff still failing? don't we do the rendering? or we should know before we do rendering right?" The preview reviewers see is quarter-res with every 4th drawing, so clipping, stills, flashing and pace were invisible until the final (warm-shoes: a face through a boot for 1 s). Now `.github/workflows/proof.yml` draws the whole film on the PR, every drawing the final makes, at half size with the real voice and mix, and fails on a craft block, the flash bar, a head through a prop or a hot peak. The qa agent then checks that proof (`"stage": "proof"` in `qa/<story>.json`, with the commit it was drawn from), and `test` blocks the merge without it. Before any render at all, `preflight` blocks on stills over 1.0 s and on clipping, read from the edit and from Blender.
  - **Every film needs a passed final-film check** (`qa/<story>.json` for that exact film, `.claude/agents/qa.md`) before `upload.py` will take it. After the proof it's a short confirm. No check means no upload that day, and that's fine.

Re-uploading a near-copy of something the channel already posted and took down risks YouTube's duplicate and reused-content signals. A pulled idea worth keeping comes back only as a new story: new script, shots and title. ep36-ice-huts stays private for good.

A crazy, strange, made-up story, as long as its big idea needs and no longer (shorter is better, 3:00 at most), told in first person by a fast, sped-up synthetic narrator over blocky characters we build in Blender, with big punchy captions. One story a day goes to **Buhtown** and no other channel (note 21).

## Hard rules (break one and the story doesn't ship)
1. **Fiction, never claimed as true.** No "true story", "this happened", "based on", "real". No real people, crimes, places, routes or street names, and that includes text and signs in the set. Keep the set free of real words: rule markers of our own design (numbers, glyphs, made-up words checked against real places) are fine.
2. **Creepy, not graphic.** Suspense and the uncanny only. No gore, killing, death words, weapons, sexual content or self-harm. No child in the cast (adults, or teens at the youngest), nobody young in peril, and nobody shown hurt.
3. **Not made for kids, every time.** The art draws young viewers, and YouTube removes kid-styled videos with mature themes. `made_for_kids` is false, and nothing in the title, description or pin aims at kids.
4. **Nothing of Roblox's own.** No assets, meshes, textures, catalogue faces, decals, UI, fonts, logo or name. Every face, pattern and mesh is made in `blocky/`. Titles, descriptions and pins never say Roblox or any other game or toy brand, not even to deny it. No character copies a specific avatar or item.
5. **Every story is new, and no two look templated** (YouTube's July 2025 monetization rules exclude "narrated stories... with only superficial differences"; `docs/research/2026-11-02-process-research.md`). YouTube's first example of mass-produced content is "narrated stories with only superficial differences", and this channel is blocky-only, so the whole channel gets read for sameness. Each story has its own world, set, cast, creature, twist and ending shape. **Line 1 varies too** (standards, 2026-11-03: the opening fixes converged on "Never…"): every `package.json` tags its `"opening"` as one of `ban` ("Never/Don't X"), `if` ("If you X, Y"), `must` ("you have to / you can't"), `count` (a count that's wrong), `confess` ("I…/My…", already in it), `cost` (the place's rule and its price) or `whatif` (the what-if lane). `upload.py` never puts two uploads in a row with the same opening, `ban` is at most 1 in 4 of a week, no other shape is over 1 in 3. Shapes and counts: `docs/research/opening-variety.md`. `check` blocks a shared set file, cast member, premise or twist, or more than 12% shared three-word phrases.
6. **The score is ours; effects may be recorded.** The score is synthesized in `blocky/sound.py`: no samples, no borrowed melody, no music box. Footsteps, ambience and effects may use the CC0 or royalty-free library in `assets/` (owner, 2026-11-02: "yes go ahead with sfx and visuals"), and every file there has a row in `assets/LICENSES.md`: never NonCommercial, never BBC Sound Effects. Visuals may use CC0 textures, HDRIs and props from `assets/` for sets and lighting (`kit.texture`, `look.hdri`, `kit.prop`: the owner's Quaternius and Kenney download, 2026-11-03); the characters, their faces and the story stay ours (hard rule 4), and no prop with lettering, a sign or a logo goes in (hard rule 1). **No downloaded creature meshes** (owner, 2026-11-03, after the creature lab: "Do whatever you think is best"). The lab's one usable CC0 horror model scored 3, no better than our own blocks, and the critic said it clashed with our cast. The free CC0 packs are mostly cute game monsters. So creatures stay built from `blocky/` parts and designed with `docs/research/creature-playbook.md`: a person made wrong, one lit identity cue, a rim, the face half in shadow, and a scale reference in the reveal. The master is -14 LUFS with true peak at or below -1 dBTP.
7. **No flashing over the house bar.** New stories set `look.flash_safe: true` (slow, smooth flicker in `kit.py`), and every light switch in `set.py` ramps over at least 0.3 s. Flickering bulbs and instant blackouts failed the bar on most stories built on 2026-11-01. **Only a real strobe blocks** (owner, 2026-11-03: "I definitely think the flash is too strict. The thing it pointed out has been like transitions and stuff. Can we just remove that check or make it much looser?"). The upload refuses a film only for sustained flashing (IRIS's extended rule: 4 s of any 5 s busy), a hard strobe (over 6 flashes in a second), red flashes over 3 a second or a full-screen red frame. Everything else the meter sees (flash and change counts, fast cuts between bright and dark shots) is a warning in the report. `python -m blocky flash` measures it, and the upload re-measures every film with the current bar. The description's "Flashing lights and fast cuts" line goes on only when a film comes near the bar (it trips a warning), not for ordinary cuts (owner, 2026-11-03, after watching closing-time and mirror-maze: "i watched them and i didnt see the flashes...").
8. **Write like a person** (note 9). No em or en dashes, semicolons, "not just X but Y", "no X, no Y", three-beat slogans, lists in parentheses, or AI words (delve, journey, fascinating, testament, unveil, "ever wondered"). One emoji at most. Read it out loud.

## The story
- **The lore is the point** (owner, 2026-11-02: "the rule is necessarily in this world, in this town, whatever, and then we have to have super deep lore, super duper interesting lore. I don't think it necessarily needs to be scary, could be like eerie. I don't think it necessarily needs to be a monster. It just needs to make them feel a certain way, and like have like, oh wow, that was like actually like movie level performance, like Christopher Nolan could never"). This outranks every story rule below it.
  - **Every story is built on one big idea:** a reveal that reframes the whole world rule and everything we saw, so the ending feels like the floor dropping away. The owner's examples, from other creators, show the depth and are never to be reused: a knocking door that turns out to be a one-way gateway to the past, and a town where things vanish to a beeping sound because the narrator is waking from a coma.
  - **The rule is the clue.** The first line's rule ("In this town, …") is the big idea seen from inside. Every strange detail is a planted piece of it, and the reveal makes them all click at once. A second watch should feel like a different film.
  - **The viewer must get it.** The reveal is said plainly, in one or two lines and one picture, in the last 20 to 30 s, then left to land. It's never left for the comments to work out, and never so vague that it reads as confusion (ep20's problem).
  - **Feeling over fright.** Eerie, sad, awed or uneasy are all fine. A monster is optional. What's required is that the idea lands as a feeling: someone the narrator loves, and what the idea means for them.
  - **The test** (critic and writer): can you say the big idea in one sentence that makes someone say "oh no" or "wait, what"? If the twist is only "the creature was keeping someone's place", it isn't big enough.
- **Shape:** the strange rule or hook in the first two seconds. Three or four strange nights or visits, each adding one concrete picture. The rule broken. A real scare in the middle. A twist planted early. Then a warm turn, shown and not told, and a last line that loops back into the first.
- **What the hits do** (owner, 2026-11-02: "Please update everything based on the stuff we've seen"). Patterns seen on at least 3 different channels across 27 hits on YouTube Shorts and Instagram (9.6M to 36M views; `docs/research/`). They're the default shape now. The critic checks each one:
  1. **The first line states one rule that touches everyone:** "In this world/town, …" plus a concrete rule, under 16 words ("In this town, you get 1,000,000 steps."). It's the genre's signature and viewers recognise it. Our creepy lane: a rule that starts as wonder or everyday life and turns eerie.
  2. **The rule is visible by 2 s,** ideally as a marker of our own design on or above every character: a number, a glyph, a glow, an object (counters, tags, bells, strings). Both format flops we studied hid the rule.
  3. **A 3 to 5 example montage in the first 15 s** shows how the rule shapes daily life, and it can be funny ("Date night is just two people walking sideways").
  4. **The narrator is the exception** to the rule (300 steps left, the one with no number, the bell that never rang).
  5. **A loved one is at stake** (a parent, grandparent, sibling or partner, always adult or teen).
  6. **The climax turns the rule's own logic against itself:** a loophole or a second reading, never new magic.
  7. **The twist is planted early and the last line echoes the first** (as above).
  8. **The first 2 s read clearly on a phone:** lit to read, the rule and a face in frame. Darkness and fog come after the hook.
  9. **Cut often:** a new shot every 1 to 2 s on average (`check` warns over 2.3 s; the reset's target is 2.0).
- **The creepy lane** (`docs/research/2026-11-02-creepy-lane-notes.md`: the 10 most-viewed creepy rule Shorts, 5.7M to 16.4M views, against 2 flops):
  - **Wonder first, then the turn.** The rule's opening image is odd or funny, not scary. The first real scare lands about a quarter to two-fifths in (about 35 to 55 s for us), usually the first person taken. Before that, the montage can be played for laughs.
  - **Show the creature little and late:** shadows, sounds and **what it leaves behind** ("Only the cone was left") until about 55 to 70% through, then clearly. It's tall and slow, leans in face to face, and never chases. That's our "never seen moving, only closer", now with a timing. Taken means gone, never shown hurt (hard rule 2).
  - **Light it so it reads when it's shown.** A creature too dark to make out was one flop's problem. The other flop never showed it at all.
  - **Keep the warm turn, but end on a sting.** Most hits resolve warm, then the last line bites: a horror loop back to the first line, or a short in-voice threat. Our loop-back last line does this. Make the final beat land cold.
  - **The music builds:** thin at first, then a heavy low drone enters at the first taking and holds to the end. A short drop before a reveal is still fine. The flops never built.
  - **Avoid the genre's tired template.** Viewers now call out "we moved in, nobody told us the rule, the mayor sends him to the square at midnight". Never use a newcomer-to-town opener, a mayor, or a midnight square summons. Our rule-breaker and our stakes are personal.
- **Studio, 2026-11-02** (`metrics/studio/2026-11-02.md`): back-row 82.6% chose to view; ep20 64.7%, ep38 61.3% and knock-back 63.1%. Every Short since back-row lost over a third at the swipe, and the feed stopped testing it within one to four hours. **Rule-first didn't fix the swipe** (ep38 and knock-back did no better than ep20). back-row's first line was a concrete, personal mystery with numbers that don't add up, and its first 15 s were rewatched (above 100%). Until the research says otherwise, the first line and first frame must pose a concrete mystery or a threat to *me*, right now, with something countable or visibly wrong. A world rule alone isn't a hook.
- **Our own numbers so far** (ep11, `docs/research/2026-11-02-our-channel-analysis.md`): 82.6% stayed to watch and the average view was 84% of 2:18. The hook and the length work. Reach is held back by shares (3) and comments (7), so the pin question and loophole-worthy rules matter. The one dip, 0:18 to 0:26, was a character explaining the rule. **Seconds 15 to 30 escalate or show the rule at work; nobody explains it.**
  - **ep20 did much worse** (owner, 2026-11-02: "Ep20 has pretty bad stats, 47% average view duration and 64% stayed to watch"). Against ep11, it opened on a vague line ("It's getting closer.") over a hand that read as a cartoon glove, with no rule. It then spent 0:03 to 0:20 on backstory (two years of not speaking, a cousin's wedding, Mum), over an empty hallway and dim velvet walls. **So: the first line is the world's rule, the threat reads as a threat at phone size in the first 2 s, and no backstory or flashback comes before 0:20.** **Rule first is required** (owner, same day: "I think it's fairly clear that a rule first is necessary. So we can do all of the ones that are rule first, and then we can like send a note to redo like all the other ones"). Every story's first line states its world's rule. A story that doesn't is held (`"hold"` in package.json) and redone before it uploads.
  - **The narrator is a man or a teen boy, always** (owner on ep38, 2026-11-02: "the narrator sounding like a guy and he says 'husband'"). Every story uses the one male narrator voice. So the narrator's own family must fit a man: my wife, girlfriend, mum, dad, brother, sister, grandparents, never "my husband" or "my boyfriend". On screen, the "I" character is male too, and women and girls are the people the narrator talks about.
  - **Never narrow the audience** (owner, 2026-11-02: "We shouldnt ever talk about anything that would decrease the possible audience size"). Every story is for everyone. No politics, religion, sexuality, current events, or any theme that splits viewers or could read as taking a side. Romance is risky, because it's easy to misread and narrows who relates. Prefer family, friends, home, school-age memories told by a teen or adult, and fears everyone shares.
  - **Back-row is the template** (owner, 2026-11-02: "There was something obviously good about the first one? at least the metrics were great"). ep11 back-row kept 82.6% and an 84% average view. What it did:
    - one plain rule as line 1 ("On the night bus, you never sit in the back row.")
    - one concrete mystery within 15 s (the driver counts six, I count five)
    - one place, one escalation (the empty back row gets worse each night)
    - no relationships to decode, and no backstory
    - a twist said plainly ("The count was right all along.")
    Every new story is checked against these five. A story may add a big idea on top, but never at the cost of being easy to follow.
  - **Relationships read at a glance, as intended** (owner on ep38, 2026-11-02: "lowkey its controversial since it reads like they are not a straight couple even though it should be... Also its confusing and idk whats going on"). A couple, parent, child or sibling is named in the narration in plain words the first time they appear ("my husband, Dom"), and looks it on screen in the same beat. A twist never rests on reading a silhouette, a haircut or a piece of clothing. ep38's twist hinged on a silhouette, and viewers read the couple as two women.
  - **The cold-viewer test** (from ep20 and ep38, both "confusing"). Before a story is built, a fresh agent that has read nothing else hears only the narration once. It must say in two plain sentences what happened and what the twist means, and who each person is to the narrator. If it can't, or it gets a relationship wrong, the story goes back to the writer.
  - **Make them feel something, and keep it clear** (owner on ep20, same day: "I didn't really like feel anything from the story. And yeah, I don't know, it was also a little bit confusing"). One timeline told forward, with no flashback opening and no second booth or second wedding to keep track of. Who the narrator loves and what they could lose is clear by 0:20, shown in a picture rather than explained, so the ending lands as a feeling.
- **What we keep from the owner's own calls** (the research doesn't override them): the same R6 body type, any length the story needs (owner, 2026-11-02: "We can keep shorter if needed too, the 2:15 to 2:30 was arbitrary"; the scope research's top examples run 11 to 76 s, and ep20 kept 47% of 2:30). Test it: build about a third of each batch at 60 to 90 s, plus **one or two ultra-shorts at 20 to 45 s** (owner, 2026-11-02: "are we ensuring to experiment too? like ex with shorter shorts?"; the trend scan's fastest POV clips run 13 to 44 s). An ultra-short is one rule, one beat and one twist, often a POV or comedy experiment. Let `metrics` compare by length, our one narrator voice and our two-or-three-word captions with a key colour (YouTube's hits mostly use phrase blocks with a coloured key word too). And no child narrators: the hits often use one, but a teen or an adult looking back is our line, because kid-styled videos with mature themes get removed.
- **Telling:** about 290 wpm while speaking, one short sentence per beat, a new shot on almost every beat. Every line is `who: narrator`, and characters are quoted inside the narration ("He looked at me and said, "Six.""). Nobody gets a voice of their own (owner, 2026-10-30).
- **The middle must grip,** scary or eerie (owner, 2026-10-31: "I low-key don't think the story is like creepy enough"; 2026-11-02: "could be like eerie"). Escalate, don't describe: something a row closer each time the lights come back, a voice copied, a head turned too far.
- **Deep lore and a real twist** (owner, 2026-11-01: "something I think is interesting is having deep story and lore, or like a twist. I think that sort of stuff is doing quite well on instagram and youtube should be right behind").
  - **The twist turns the whole story over.** The last 15 seconds make the first line mean something new, so a second watch plays differently. Plant it at least twice where a rewatch catches it. A twist that only adds a fact isn't one.
  - **The shared world is optional now** (2026-11-02: the owner saw no lore in ep20, whose lore was a Keeper's tally hidden in the background). Each story's own big idea comes first. A background link to `stories/lore.md` is allowed, never required, and never stands in for the big idea. The old note follows. `stories/lore.md` is the canon: what's under the town, who keeps the rules, what the creatures are to each other. Each story is still complete on its own, with its own setting, creature and ending. It plants **one** small connection to the canon (a symbol, a name, a sound, an object in the background) that only a repeat viewer spots. It's never needed to follow the story, and it's never a cliffhanger. (Part 2s are now allowed, see the scope research notes.)
  - **The pin can invite theories** ("what was on the back of the ticket?"). The answer is in the canon, never in the comments.
  - The lore never makes two stories alike: rule 5 still holds for setting, creature, twist and ending shape.
  - **The canon isn't the twist.** `stand-in` (a Keeper keeps someone's empty place until they come back) is already in more than ten stories. Pick a twist that turns the story over on its own, and let the Keeper fit around it. `stories/lore.md` → "Other things a count can do" lists shapes the canon supports.
- **Vary the warmth** (owner, 2026-10-31: "We can have other heartwarming endings"): friendship, loyalty, helping, home. Never again "the monster was kind/lonely all along" or the ritual done back.
- **Scope research** (`docs/research/2026-11-02-scope-research.md`, owner's research, 2026-11-02). These are hypotheses to test, not settled rules:
  - **Judge on volume:** make about 20 Shorts in 4 weeks before deciding anything. One weak upload (ep20) is a signal, not a verdict.
  - **The premise is the product,** not the setting. Bus, train and elevator settings already have 2 to 8M hits from big channels, and settings with no premise found nothing. This backs rule first and the big idea above.
  - **An eerie twist on a premise beats straight horror** (eerie premises reach 10 to 33M, plain animated horror stays under 1M).
  - **Study Gneef, Noobified and PurpleHat on YouTube directly.** Many Instagram "hits" in our first report were re-uploads of them. Never copy one of their premises: their audience has seen them.
  - **Decided by the owner** (2026-11-02):
    - **Two uploads a day, while the queue allows** (owner, 2026-11-02: "Can we try to upload twice a day? how do we ensure that youtube doesnt block us and think we are a bot?"; this replaces "one a day, no faster"). They go public at 4:00 PM and 7:30 PM Central plus a few random minutes. The second is skipped whenever fewer than 5 ready stories would stay queued. Staying clear of spam signals: every title, description and story is unique, never templated or near-duplicate; uploads are hours apart, never bursts; nothing misleading in the metadata; and the API is used only to upload, read and comment. The batch size rises to keep about 14 a week coming, within the usage limit.
    - **Titles state the rule in title case, with one word in caps** (owner: "We can tweak the titles thats fine"), as in the research's hits: "In This Town The Bus NEVER Stops". Under 60 characters where possible, no question titles, never the reveal. **Vary the opening words:** "In This Town" may start at most a third of a week's titles. Use the place itself ("On This Night Bus...", "At This Lake...", "In This Aquarium..."), the rule as a statement ("Nobody Sits in the BACK Row"), or a short noun phrase like the big channels use ("The Bell That RINGS Before Mistakes"). The first spoken line still states the rule.
    - **A closer-to-Roblox look is fine** ("Closer to roblox is fine"). The scope research found that non-Roblox-looking 3D stories rarely surface, so the look itself may be part of what gets these recommended. Closer means the game's general feel, built by us: bright, saturated plastic colours, smooth slightly glossy plastic, flat grey baseplate and simple grass ground, game-style sky and fog, and stiff R6-style poses and walks. Hard rule 4 still holds: no Roblox asset, mesh, texture, catalogue or default face, UI, font, logo or name, and no copied avatar or item. Test it: in the next batches, build about half the stories with `"look": {"style": "closer"}` in story.json and half in our current style, so `metrics` can compare. The first builder to use it builds the style once in `blocky/` as a shared preset (palette, plastic material, ground, sky), and every later story reuses it, so the test compares one look against the other.
    - **Part 2s are allowed** ("Yes try a part 2, but it needs to make sense on its own too and be entertaining on its own, while still being entertaining to people who've watched part 1"). A Part 2 is a full story with its own rule-first opening, big idea and ending. It works for someone who never saw Part 1, and it rewards someone who did with a callback or a deeper turn of the same idea. Make one for a premise once it clearly beats the channel's median at day 7.
    - **A weekly compilation** (owner, 2026-11-02: "Once a week, also i dont think that it should count as an upload, it cna be separate. the elngth is around 20 minutes. the thumnail is like a bunch of the stories stitched side by side"). On Sundays, `scripts/compile.py` joins about 20 minutes of published stories into one 16:9 long video. Each story is centred on a blurred copy of itself, there's a chapter per story, and the thumbnail is the stories' covers side by side. The title is "In This Town / In This World: Creepy Story Animation Compilation (N min)", never naming the game. It goes public at noon Central and doesn't count as the day's upload. Long-form views pay more and count toward the 4,000 watch hours.
    - **No #roblox tag** on videos ("we don't need the roblox tag"). The owner may list it in the channel's keywords. Titles, descriptions and pins still never name it.
- **Never reuse what batch 1 shared:** a knowing elder who explains in cryptic quotes. A night-shift or one-summer narrator. A pale grey blank-faced creature. "Pressed FLAT" against glass. A closing-section opener like "Last NIGHT,". An on-screen age. "It didn't touch me". "Not X, it was Y" cadences.
- **Within a batch** no two stories share a setting, creature type, rule type (what the world's rule is about) or kind of warmth (ending shapes may repeat, owner 2026-11-02). `stories/ideas.md` lists what's been used.
- **Read every line against the picture under it.** Watch for self-contradictions ("TALL... WIDER than it's TALL"), a count that doesn't match the knocks we hear, and a line about skin landing on a character it doesn't fit.
- **Titles** state the world's rule as a statement, in title case with one word in caps (see the owner's decisions above). A warning is fine since the 2026-11-03 research ("Never Cross This Bridge With DARK Hands": 7 of the 17 fastest story Shorts have warning titles, `docs/research/playbook.md`), but not a bare instruction that reads like a game hint ("When the floor knocks, knock back" was wrong), and at most one "Never" title in any 7 uploads (hard rule 5). They never spoil the reveal or reuse an earlier title's shape.
- **Volume** (owner, 2026-11-02: "we should just be trying to pump out as many as possible right now and then I'll just rate limit and upload once per day manually"): make as many stories as we can in parallel sessions. Similar ending shapes across a batch are fine (owner, same day: "We don't need to limit how many are like that"), but the hard rules and the originality check still apply. The owner paces the uploads, one a day.

- **No harsh sound effects** (owner, 2026-11-02, ep36: "there's some horrible like audio quality stuff... a horrible scratchy sound"). The grass footsteps were bright noise bursts and read as scratching. Effects sit low and soft (under about 4 kHz) unless the story needs a sting. The critic listens for hiss, scratch and clicks on every preview. Since 2026-11-03 steps and ambience are soft recordings from `assets/audio/` (owner, 2026-11-02: "yes go ahead with sfx and visuals"), kept under 5% of their energy above 4 kHz.
- **Few effects, music under everything** (owner, 2026-11-03, watching the fast-rising Shorts with sound: "It doesnt look like very many sound effects, but we can probably do them to make it seem higher quality. also there is background music."). Our own synthesized score (hard rule 6) plays from the first frame to the last, under the voice, with holes only at the `music_drops` before a scare. Effects are few and sell a moment: the opening hit, real impacts on screen, doors and steps we see, and the riser into the twist. No whoosh on plain cuts. Aim for 2 to 4 sound events per 10 s in all, the builder's own included (`python scripts/measure.py --story stories/<name>` counts them). The polish pack does its part by default (v2.2).

## The look
- **The polish pack is on for every new story** (owner, 2026-11-03: "make them high quality or keep pushing the quality up... we cant upload stuff we know wont work or be good. the bar should be very high."). Set `"look": {"polish": true}` in story.json (`docs/engine.md` → The polish pack). It adds soft whooshes between lines, low hits on key words, risers into the silences before scares, a room bed, voice-first ducking, punch-ins so something changes every 1 to 2 s, drift on held shots, a quick caption pop and a bright grade that keeps shadows on a TV. On ep32-sitter the critic scored it better on sound, pace and picture and no worse anywhere (`docs/research/polish-pack.md`). Mark `quiet` over a warm beat that should stay still.
- **Craft is measured, not judged by eye alone** (owner, 2026-11-03: "we should care a lot about the micro and the macro. Like making it so the animations feel real and clean and good and transitions and effects on top, making it look higher quality. we should do research on what this actually looks like, and actually measure it, not just vibes."). `scripts/measure.py` measures every final (cuts, picture changes, stills, camera and character motion, easing, brightness, faces, captions, sound events, the bed, loudness, harshness), and `qa` pastes its table into `qa/<story>.json`. The browser study of 17 hits (2026-11-03) confirmed four, which block for films drawn from 2026-11-03 6:00 PM Central: at least 12.5 picture changes per 10 s (0.5 s samples) and 14 at 0.25 s, motion energy at least 6, and no picture still for more than 1.0 s. The hits change the picture about twice as often as we do. The rest warn (`docs/research/craft-metrics.md`). **A near miss isn't a block** (owner, 2026-11-03, on ep48-crazy-golf, dropped at 12.0, 13.7 and 5.78 after both reviews approved it: "all of these seem very close... like there is wiggle room" and "We don't necessarily know what is the best but those seem pretty close...", then "band can be like 10%"). A film that misses a pace bar (picture changes at 0.5 s or 0.25 s, motion energy) by 10% or less reads `near`: the proof passes, and qa watches the slowest 10 s at phone size and fails it only if something there drags. Each bar is the hits' p10, the same film measured two ways differs by up to 0.9 picture changes, and a flop still fails on its picture changes. Longest still, flash, clips and loudness have no band. Builders can run it with `--story` alone before a render, and the proof render (above) runs the whole table on the PR. The biggest gaps today: every walk starts and stops dead, characters hold still in about half the shots, and most frames are drawn on twos.
- **Characters** (`blocky/rig.py`, `faces.py`): R6 blocky bodies, a soft rounded-cylinder head, flat drawn faces with thick black strokes, block hair, hats, patterned shirts. A face at phone size is at least 1/5 of the frame width, out of the caption band.
  - **The body type stays** (owner, 2026-11-01: "I kinda want them to be the same body type"). Research found that Roblox's terms restrict off-platform commercial use of "derivatives of the classic Roblox avatars" (R6/R15), and our proportions match R6 (`docs/research/2026-11-01-web-research.md`). The owner accepted that risk. Keep it small everywhere else: every face, hair piece, outfit and pattern is our own, never a catalogue item or the default face, and there's never the name, logo or UI anywhere.
- **Sets:** realistic materials around the plastic avatars (brick, wood, cobbles, concrete), practical lights that flicker, long shadows, game-engine fog. Each story's `set.py` is new.
- **The creature, when there is one** (a monster is optional, owner 2026-11-02). If a story has one, it has to be good:
  - It's built from the same blocks as everyone else, but wrong: too tall, too wide or too flat, limbs too long, a face blank, upside down or opening onto darkness. It's never seen moving, only closer. Thin rods and tentacles read as props (owner: "the tentacle just looks silly").
  - It's one model, built once in `set.py` and posed per shot, so it's the same in every shot.
  - **Test still first.** Render one finished, phone-size still of the reveal before building anything else, and iterate until it's frightening, not silly.
  - **Light it for fear:** front lights dead, a key from below or the side, a rim on the silhouette, no clipped white. The eye highlights are the brightest thing.
- **Captions:** Schibsted Grotesk Black at 84 px, white with key words in CAPS and the story's own colour, two or three words, each one appearing on its own first word (sync beats hold time).
- **Voice:** Kokoro `am_adam`, tempo 1.03, sped up 1.45 times (the owner's pick, "Voice A sounds the best"). It's matched by ear, never cloned from anyone.
- **Sound:** drop everything but the voice and the hit for 0.5 to 0.8 s before a scare (`music_drops`). A closing sound needs -18 dB or a pitch fall to be heard. Leave 0.2 to 0.3 s between the last word and the loop cut.

## The rig's limits (plan shots around them)
- R6 hands can't grip: cut away instead of showing a hand on a wheel or a knock.
- No cross-legged sitting and no reach to the floor from a chair.
- Seated riders are about 1.36 m wide, so space seats for that.
- Long arms foreshortened toward camera read as beams: show the hand at the end of the sleeve.
- Check every creature beat and every pose near the set for intersections in the stills before the final.

## Packaging (`stories/<story>/package.json`)
```json
{"title": "...", "description": "...", "pin": "...", "made_for_kids": false, "cover_s": 107.0, "shape": "stand-in"}
```
- **Title:** under 100 characters, by the rules above.
- **Description:** two or three short paragraphs. It sets up the story without spoiling it, says "It's made up.", and can say it's "built with code and Blender". The upload adds the measured flashing line on top by itself. Never: game or toy names, true, real, based on, kid, teen, child, baby, school, AI voice, no AI, made by hand, Kokoro, another franchise's monster.
- **Title:** state the world's rule, the way the hits do ("In this town, everyone has a price"). It's a statement or a warning (see the title rule above), and it never spoils the twist.
- **Pin (since the 2026-11-03 reset):** "Comment WORD if..." with one easy word tied to the story. Before that it was a question the viewer answers about themselves under the rule ("How many lies would your number show?"), or one about a detail planted early ("would you have sat back there?"). The owner posts it as their own first comment and pins it: on YouTube, the creator's own question sits in the top comments of 8 of the 15 Shorts studied.
- **Cover (`cover_s`):** the second of the frame that becomes the thumbnail. Pick the creature or the rule marker clearly lit, with a face if possible and the caption tying back to the title (ep20: 1:47, "stood in the MIDDLE."). Without it, the render picks 78% of the way in. The upload issue shows the cover, and the owner picks that moment in the YouTube app.
- **Hold (`hold`):** `"hold": "why"` keeps a story out of the daily upload until it's redone. Remove it when the redo passes review.
- **Shape (`shape`):** a short tag for the twist's shape, such as `stand-in` (a Keeper keeps a missing person's place until they come back), `never-left`, `trick`, `about-me`, `double`, `misread` or `rule-logic`. Reuse a tag when the shape matches, or coin a new one. The daily upload never puts two stories with the same shape back to back while another one is ready (2026-11-02: ten stories in the parallel batches share `stand-in`).
- No hashtags or tags for now. Altered or synthetic content: No (it's animated fiction).

## Budget and craft
- $0: free tools only. Renders run on GitHub Actions (free for this public repo), and uploads use the free YouTube Data API.
- The preview is quarter-res with every 4th drawing, so always say which one you're showing. The final is 540x960, sharpened to 1080x1920.
